Station 1 · Lymphatics

Case. 26-year-old with left arm swelling for 3 months after a breast lumpectomy with axillary lymph node removal.

The 14-minute station, in order
  1. Introduce yourself, say why you are examining, wash hands.
  2. Ask the chief complaint, then 2 pertinent questions (e.g. "When did the swelling start?" "Any pain, redness or fever?").
  3. Say 1 possible diagnosis (lymphedema after axillary node removal) and ask permission to examine.
  4. Say you would do a full physical exam and name the 3 most relevant systems (lymphatic/skin, musculoskeletal, cardiovascular).
  5. Structural exam, saying each step out loud: observation, palpation (at least 5 of the 10 items), ROM.
  6. The examiner gives you ONE special test: do it, say what it is for and what a positive result looks like.
  7. Assessment and plan: medical diagnosis, your SD findings with at least one TART finding, and a treatment plan that offers OMT.
  8. Ask for your technique. The examiner gives you ONE. Explain it in plain words, why it helps, get consent right before you start.
  9. Treat, then reassess ("this feels looser now"), say what to expect ("may be sore today, better tomorrow"), give an exercise and follow-up.

Exam

Observation

Exam step

What it is for. Look for signs that lymph is not draining from the arm, and for posture problems that block drainage.

General
Head position, posture type, shoulders and shoulder blades, how the chest moves with breathing, iliac crest and leg position.
Curves
Front-back and side curves; screen for scoliosis and a rib hump.
Arms
Compare both arms and hands. Look at the skin and the surgical scar. Look for redness, wounds, swollen veins, and axillary cording (tight cords in the armpit, called axillary web syndrome).

Palpation: lymphatic assessment

Exam step

Perform and say at least 5 of these 10.

What it is for. Find the places where lymph flow is blocked. Lymph drains from the arm, through the armpit, into the veins at the base of the neck (the thoracic inlet), so that path is checked first.

1
Arm: temperature, tenderness, pitting (does a finger press leave a dent?), tissue texture, and whether the scar moves.
2
Neck: front neck fascia, the muscles along the back of the neck, and the scalenes.
3
Armpit: feel for lumps or cords.
4
Circulation and nerves: pulses, capillary refill, sensation, and strength.
5
Thoracic inlet: bogginess above the collarbone and the first rib. Test Sibson's fascia (the fascial dome over the lung top): hands placed as for a first-rib exam, test which way it rotates and sidebends more easily.
6
Sympathetic (VSR) screen T1 to L2, seated: back of the hand for temperature, muscle tone, skin drag for swelling, deeper pressure for spindle-shaped (fusiform) edema, then the red reflex: stroke both sides of the spine; red that stays = acute, red that fades fast = chronic. Levels: T1–T4 head and neck, T1–T6 heart and lungs, T2–T7 arm, T5–T9 upper gut, T10–T11 middle gut, T12–L2 lower gut, T10–L2 leg.
7
Pectoralis minor on both sides.
8
Diaphragm, supine: bucket-handle motion of ribs 6 to 10 at the mid-axillary line; caliper motion of ribs 11 and 12 with your hands under the back; spring the L1 to L3 TPs (where the diaphragm crura attach); then thumbs under the costal margins as the patient breathes in. Say which hemidiaphragm is restricted.
Sinuses
Seated, patient leaning forward: tap over the frontal and maxillary sinuses and ask about tenderness and where it spreads.
9
Pelvic floor: patient prone, press just inside the sitting bones (medial ischial tuberosities).
10
Popliteal fossa (back of the knee): fullness and tone, using TART.

Range of motion

Exam step

What it is for. Check whether the shoulder, upper back and ribs move freely, because stiff ribs and a stiff upper back slow the pumping of lymph.

Active
Shoulders on both sides: flexion, extension, internal and external rotation, abduction, adduction. Compare sides.
Thoracic spine
Patient seated: spring front-to-back on the upper, middle and lower segments.
First rib
Spring the first rib to feel how it moves.
Ribs
Ribs 1 to 5 move mostly like a pump handle (front goes up and down). Ribs 6 to 10 move mostly like a bucket handle (side goes up and down). Feel both as the patient breathes.

Special tests (examiner picks one)

Bilateral circumference measurements

Special test

What it is for. Measures how much bigger the swollen arm is, and gives a number to follow over time.

How
Use a tape measure around both arms at the same marked levels (for example, a set distance above and below the elbow, and at the wrist). Compare the two sides.
Positive
The affected arm is larger. A difference of about 2 cm or more at any level supports lymphedema.

No pictures of this one in the course or SGA material.

Axillary cord assessment

Special test

What it is for. Looks for axillary web syndrome: tight, rope-like cords of scarred lymph vessels after node surgery.

How
Abduct the shoulder (lift the arm out to the side) and straighten the elbow. Look at and feel the armpit and inner arm.
Positive
A visible or palpable tight cord, often painful, that limits lifting the arm.

No pictures of this one in the course or SGA material.

Stemmer sign

Special test

What it is for. A quick bedside test for lymphedema.

How
Try to gently pinch and lift a fold of skin on the back of the base of the second finger.
Positive
You cannot lift the skin fold. The skin is thick and stuck down, which means lymphedema. A negative sign does not rule it out.

No pictures of this one in the course or SGA material.

Assessment and plan

What to document

Exam step

What it is for. Tie your findings together before you treat.

Medical diagnosis
Secondary lymphedema of the left arm after axillary lymph node removal.
SD findings
With at least one TART finding: thoracic spine SD (Type I and/or Type II), 11th and 12th rib diagnosis, thoracic inlet fascial restriction, and which diaphragm is most restricted (the thoracoabdominal diaphragm on the left or right, the pelvic floor, or the popliteal fossa).
Plan
Offer OMT aimed at the SDs, to improve comfort, breathing, motion and lymph flow, together with standard lymphedema care (compression, exercise, skin care).

Techniques (examiner picks one)

Order of a full lymphatic treatment: open the central drains first, then work outward. Rib raising, thoracic inlet, diaphragm, pectoral lift, thoracic pump, cervical and sinus effleurage, then pedal pump.

1

Seated rib raising

Technique

What it is for. Calms overactive sympathetic nerves (the sympathetic chain ganglia lie right in front of the rib heads), frees the ribs, and helps breathing and lymph flow.

Patient
Seated, arms crossed, leaning forward with the crossed arms or head resting on your shoulder.
You
Sit or stand in front of the patient, facing them.
Contact
Reach under the arms and around to the back. Put your fingertips on the rib angles on both sides, just medial to the angle.
Applicator
Finger pads of both hands; your forearms act as a lever.
Force / direction
Pull the rib angles forward (anterior) and outward (lateral) by leaning your body backward, so the ribs lift. Hold 20 to 30 seconds, or repeat in a slow rhythm until the ribs move better.
Levels
Work up and down the ribs. Upper ribs (T1 to T4) affect the head, neck and arms; lower ribs (T7 to T10) affect the abdomen.

Do not use with: Acute rib fracture, unstable heart rhythm, bowel obstruction.

2

Thoracic inlet myofascial release (direct or indirect)

Technique

What it is for. The thoracic inlet (T1, the first ribs and the collarbones) is where all lymph enters the veins. Freeing its fascia opens the main drain, so it is treated first.

Check first
Look for and feel T1 SD, a stiff first rib, and swelling above the collarbone. Then treat, then recheck.
Patient
Seated (it can also be done supine).
You
Stand behind the patient.
Contact
Broad hand contact: thumbs on the back over T1 and its TPs and the first rib; fingers draped forward over the collarbones and first ribs.
Force / direction
Test 3 motions: rotation (clockwise or counterclockwise), sidebending (left or right), and bringing your hands together or apart. Indirect: move the tissue into the easy direction of all 3 and stack them. Direct: move into the tight direction of all 3.
Hold
Hold while the patient breathes slowly in and out, and wait until you feel the tissue soften and let go. Then recheck.

3

Re-doming the thoracoabdominal diaphragm (or one hemidiaphragm)

Technique

What it is for. The diaphragm is the main pump for lymph. A flat, tight diaphragm moves less, so lymph moves less. Re-doming restores its dome shape and its up-and-down motion.

Diagnose
Thumbs under the costal margins: which side resists more as you press up, and which side moves less when the patient breathes in? Also check L1 to L3 (where the diaphragm crura attach) and the 12th rib.
Patient
Supine with knees bent so the belly is relaxed. Or seated, slumped slightly forward, with you standing behind and curling your fingers under the costal margins.
Contact
Thumbs or fingers just under the costal margins, pointing up toward the head.
Force / direction
When the patient breathes out, follow the diaphragm up toward the head. When the patient breathes in, gently resist it moving down.
Dose
3 to 4 breaths, or repeat until you feel it release. For a hemidiaphragm, work on the more restricted side.

4

Pectoral lift

Technique

What it is for. Stretches the pectoral muscles and fascia, which lifts the chest wall, helps the ribs move with breathing, and helps lymph drain from the armpit and chest.

Patient
Supine.
You
Sit or stand at the head of the table.
Contact
Firmly grasp the outer edge of the pectoral muscles at the front of the armpits (anterior axillary folds), with your fingertips under the muscle. Stay at the armpit fold, not on breast tissue.
Force / direction
Lean back slowly to pull the pectorals up toward the ceiling and the head (superior and anterior).
Breathing
Ask the patient to breathe deeply. As they breathe in, increase the stretch. As they breathe out, keep steady traction and do not let go.
Finish
Keep going until the tissue stops giving, then let go slowly.

Do not use with: Acute rib fracture.

5

Thoracic (Miller) pump

Technique

What it is for. Changes the pressure inside the chest in rhythm, which pulls lymph centrally through the thoracic duct and cisterna chyli (the main lymph collecting sac). Used for lung infections, after surgery, and for swelling.

Patient
Supine.
You
Stand at the head of the table.
Contact
Thenar eminences just below the collarbones, fingers spread over the upper ribs toward the armpits. For a female patient, place the hands closer to the midline, over the sternum.
Force / direction
Push the upper chest down and toward the feet (posterior and inferior) in a quick rhythm, about 2 pushes per second (about 100 per minute), strong enough that the belly moves up and down.
Breathing version
Pump only while the patient breathes out. While they breathe in, keep steady pressure on the chest. Repeat for several breaths.
Release
At the start of the last breath in, let go suddenly. The chest springs open, which pulls lymph and venous blood back fast.
Dose
About 30 seconds to 2 minutes, as tolerated.

Do not use with: Rib fracture, pneumothorax, severe osteoporosis, lung cancer, pulmonary embolism, acute heart failure.

6

Sinus drainage effleurage (air sinus drainage)

Technique

What it is for. Effleurage is light stroking in the direction lymph flows. On the face it helps the sinuses and face drain (sinus congestion, colds).

Patient
Supine.
You
Sit at the head of the table.
Contact
Finger pads of both hands, light pressure, skin only.
Order
If you are assigned only sinus drainage, go straight to the strokes. In a full lymphatic treatment, open the thoracic inlet first.
Strokes
3 to 4 sweeps in each area, always from the middle outward: 1) forehead (frontal sinus), from midline out; 2) above the eyes (supraorbital), from the inner corner of the eye along the eyebrow; 3a) below the eyes (infraorbital), along the cheekbone ridge; 3b) under the cheekbone (inframalar), from the side of the nose out; 3c) along the jaw (mandibular); 4) under the jaw (submandibular), from midline out, then down the neck.

7

Cervical lymphatic effleurage

Technique

What it is for. Moves lymph from the head and neck down toward the thoracic inlet, where it drains into the veins.

Patient
Supine.
You
Sit at the head of the table.
Contact
Flat fingers or palms, light pressure on the skin.
Force / direction
Stroke gently from behind and below the ears down along the sides of the neck (along the sternocleidomastoid) toward the collarbones. Always stroke downward, in the direction lymph flows.
Dose
Several slow, light sweeps. Open the thoracic inlet first so the lymph has somewhere to go.

8

Pedal pump (Dalrymple)

Technique

What it is for. A whole-body lymph pump. Rhythmic pushes on the feet send a wave up the body that moves lymph everywhere.

Patient
Supine.
You
Stand at the foot of the table.
Contact
Palms on the soles of both feet, fingers over the toes.
Force / direction
Dorsiflex both feet (push the toes toward the head) to the barrier. Then lean into your palms in a rhythm, about 2 pushes per second, until the belly and face gently move with each push. Not too fast, not too slow.
Other way
Hold the tops of the feet and pull them rhythmically into plantar flexion (toes pointing away).
Dose
30 seconds to 2 minutes, as tolerated.

Do not use with: Acute ankle sprain, deep vein thrombosis, acute heart failure, lymphatic cancer, recent abdominal surgery.

Also in the Practical 2 slides (not on the rubric list)

Muscle energy for ribs 11 and 12

Extra

What it is for. Ribs 11 and 12 move like calipers (back and out when breathing in). The QL pulls rib 12 down; the latissimus dorsi pulls rib 11 up. This treats a rib stuck in exhalation or inhalation.

Diagnose
Patient prone. Fingers on ribs 11 and 12 (find the free tip at the side, follow it back). Feel each side as the patient breathes. A rib that will not go up and out when breathing in is an exhalation SD.
Patient
Prone. Pull both legs toward you so the treated side is stretched open.
You
Stand on the side opposite the rib you are treating.
Exhalation SD
Put the patient's arm on that side above their head (this tightens the latissimus dorsi). Your near hand grasps the ASIS on that side and pulls the pelvis back toward you. Your other palm sits on the inside (medial) edge of ribs 11 and 12. As the patient breathes in deeply, push the ribs outward (lateral) so the breath carries them into inhalation.
Inhalation SD
Same position, but the arm stays down and the legs are moved slightly away from you (this slackens the QL). Contact the inside (medial) edge of the rib and push it outward (lateral) while the patient breathes OUT; hold it there as they breathe in.
Finish
Repeat 3 to 5 breaths, then recheck.

Exercise to give the patient

Rubric: choose one
  • Arm elevation with gentle fist pumping (open and close the hand) to push lymph up the arm.
  • Slow, deep belly breathing (diaphragmatic breathing) several times a day.
  • Self pedal pump: lie down and rock the feet back and forth (point and flex).

Station 2 · Lumbar spine

Case. 22-year-old with low back pain for 2 weeks.

The 14-minute station, in order
  1. Introduce yourself, say why you are examining, wash hands.
  2. Ask the chief complaint, then 2 pertinent questions (e.g. "Does the pain go down your leg?" "Any numbness, weakness, or trouble with bladder or bowel?").
  3. Say 1 possible diagnosis (e.g. lumbar strain, psoas syndrome, disc herniation) and ask permission to examine.
  4. Say you would do a full physical exam and name the 3 most relevant systems (musculoskeletal, neurologic, abdominal/genitourinary).
  5. Structural exam, saying each step out loud: observation, palpation, ROM.
  6. The examiner gives you ONE special test: do it, say what it is for and what a positive result looks like.
  7. Diagnosis: the lumbar SD (Type I or II) plus at least one muscle or disc finding, with at least one TART finding.
  8. If the examiner names a diagnosis and a side (for example, right QL hypertonic), tie each test to it out loud: "for a tight right QL I would expect a positive right hip drop." Say what you actually find even if it does not fit.
  9. Ask for your technique. The examiner gives you ONE. Explain it in plain words, why it helps, get consent right before you start.
  10. Treat, then reassess, say what to expect, give an exercise and follow-up.

Exam

Observation

Exam step

What it is for. Posture and gait often tell you which muscle or structure is involved before you touch the patient.

General
Posture type, breathing pattern, gait (painful or normal), toe and heel walking.
Antalgic posture
A protective lean. A tight psoas bends the patient forward and sidebends toward the tight side; a disc patient often leans away from the painful side.
Curves
Front-back and side curves; screen for scoliosis and rib hump.
Pelvis
Iliac crest heights and waist creases. Standing leg position: hip flexion/extension and rotation, and how the lumbar spine compensates.
Skin
Look for cafe-au-lait spots, moles, or a tuft of hair over the low back (a sign of spina bifida occulta).
Forward bending
Look for a rib hump or a prominent muscle bulge beside the lumbar spine.
Standing flexion test
Thumbs on both PSISs; the patient bends forward. The PSIS that rides up higher marks the side of an iliosacral SD.

Palpation

Exam step

What it is for. Find the tender, tight or stuck levels and muscles.

TART
Feel along the lumbar spine for tissue texture change, asymmetry, restriction and tenderness.
Red reflex
Stroke both sides of the spine. Red that stays = acute; red that fades fast = chronic. T10 to L2 can reflect the kidneys and ureters (VSR).
Diaphragm screen
Thumbs under the costal margins.
Muscles
Erector spinae, QL, piriformis, psoas, gluteus maximus, quadriceps, hamstrings. QL: between the iliac crest and rib 12, just lateral to and deep to the edge of the erector spinae. Piriformis: between a point one finger-width below the PSIS and the greater trochanter. Psoas: patient supine with heels toward the buttocks, press deep just medial to the ASIS; a small lift of the knee confirms you are on it.
Multifidus triangle
The triangle from the L4 SP to the iliac crest (near the PSIS) to the tip of the sacrum. It holds the multifidus muscles, the L4–L5 discs, the interspinous and intertransverse ligaments, the facet joints, the iliolumbar ligament and the sacroiliac joint, where most back problems start.

Range of motion

Exam step

What it is for. Shows how much the lumbar spine moves and which direction hurts or is blocked.

Active
Patient bends forward, backward, sidebends, and rotates; hold the hips steady and describe the curve in each position. During forward bending keep your fingers on the SPs: they should spread apart (gap). A level that does not gap is restricted in flexion. A normal lordosis flattens out when the patient bends forward.
Passive A/P spring
Seated or prone: spring front-to-back on each of the 5 lumbar vertebrae and name the level that is stiffest.
Passive, side-lying
Patient on one side, hips and knees bent to 90 degrees. Put your fingers between the SPs and check L1, L2, L3, L4 and L5 one at a time while you rock the legs forward (flexion) and back (extension). Most flexion happens at L5–S1, then L4–L5. Pain or less motion at one level means an SD there. To find L1, follow the 12th rib in to T12 and go down one; the iliac crests line up with L4–L5. For a tight QL, also lift the ankles (sidebends toward the up side) and lower them (the other side) and feel the end feel.

Lumbar SD diagnosis (Type I or Type II)

Exam step

What it is for. Names the stuck vertebra and its direction, which you need to pick and set up the treatment.

Find it
Thumbs on the TPs at each lumbar level. The side where the TP is more posterior (pushes back into your thumb) is the side the vertebra is rotated to.
Test flexion and extension
Seated: have the patient bend forward, then sit up straight or arch back. Prone: compare lying flat with propped up on the elbows (sphinx). Or spring on the TPs to bring the segment into flexion or extension.
Read it
The TP evens out in flexion and is worse in extension = flexed SD (e.g. L3 FRS right). The TP evens out in extension and is worse in flexion = extended SD (e.g. L3 ERS right). No change, over 3 or more levels, sidebent and rotated opposite = Type I (neutral) group.
Say both
Check every level from L1 to L5. State a Type I and a Type II finding; if you find no Type II, give an example of one.

Special tests (examiner picks one)

Ferguson's angle

Special test

What it is for. The lumbosacral angle. It shows how steep the top of the sacrum is, which sets how much shear stress lands on L5–S1.

How to measure
On a standing side-view X-ray, draw a horizontal line and a line along the top surface of the sacrum (the sacral base). The angle between them is Ferguson's angle.
Normal
25 to 35 degrees.
Sacrum
Sacral flexion (base tips forward) makes the angle bigger and the lordosis deeper. Sacral extension (base tips back) makes it smaller.
Why it matters
A bigger angle puts more shear and facet stress on L5–S1 and causes low back pain.

Hip drop test

Special test

What it is for. Tests lumbar sidebending.

How
Sit on a stool behind the patient so your eyes are level with the low back. Hands on top of both iliac crests. Say: "Put all your weight on your right leg, keep both heels on the floor, and bend your left knee." Then do the other side.
Normal
The hip on the bent-knee side drops 20 to 25 degrees, and the lumbar spine makes a smooth curve that sidebends AWAY from the dropped hip.
Positive
Less than 20 to 25 degrees of drop, or a curve that is not smooth. The side that does NOT drop is the positive side: the QL there is probably tight, holding that side of the pelvis up, and the lumbar spine is likely sidebent toward it. A poor left hip drop means right sidebending is restricted.

Spinous process percussion

Special test

What it is for. Screens for serious bone causes of back pain.

How
Tap each lumbar SP with a reflex hammer or your fingertips.
Positive
Sharp pain on tapping one SP points to a fracture or infection (or a tumor). Do not manipulate; get imaging.

No pictures of this one in the course or SGA material.

Iliolumbar ligament spring test

Special test

What it is for. Checks the iliolumbar ligament, which runs from the L4 and L5 TPs to the back of the iliac crest and is a common source of low back pain.

Where
In the angle between the L4–L5 TPs and the iliac crest, just above and medial to the PSIS, inside the multifidus triangle.
How
Keep two fingers on the L4 and L5 TPs (where the ligament starts) and press down (spring) on the iliac crest to stretch the ligament. Do both sides and compare. Ask about pain, and pain spreading to the groin.
Positive
Tenderness that reproduces the patient's pain = iliolumbar ligament strain.

Leg length discrepancy

Special test

What it is for. A short leg tilts the sacral base and makes the lumbar spine sidebend to compensate.

Standing
Compare iliac crest heights and the floor-to-crest level on both sides.
Supine
Measure from the ASIS to the medial malleolus (inner ankle bone) on each side with a tape. A difference means a true (anatomic) short leg.
Positive
One leg measures shorter. A short leg with equal tape measurements is a functional short leg (from the pelvis or muscle pull, like a tight QL or psoas).

Sources: Hoppenfeld, Physical Examination of the Spine and Extremities (SGA)

Straight leg raise, well leg raise, Bragard

Special test

What it is for. Stretches the sciatic nerve roots (L4 to S1) to find nerve root irritation, most often from a herniated disc.

Straight leg raise
Patient supine. Lift the straight leg slowly by the heel. Positive: pain shooting below the knee between about 30 and 70 degrees. Pain only in the back of the thigh is just tight hamstrings.
Bragard
Lower the leg just below the point of pain, then dorsiflex the foot (pull the toes up). Pain coming back confirms it is the nerve (dural sign).
Well leg raise
Lift the OTHER (painless) leg. Pain in the affected leg strongly suggests a disc herniation.

Special test

What it is for. Finds a tight hip flexor (iliopsoas), as in psoas syndrome.

How
Patient supine. The patient grabs behind one knee and pulls that thigh to the chest (you can help). Watch the OTHER leg, the one being tested.
Normal

Positive

Seated neuro exam: L4, L5, S1 (+ Babinski)

Special test

What it is for. Finds which nerve root is pinched.

L4
Motor: foot inversion (tibialis anterior). Reflex: patellar (knee jerk). Sensation: inner (medial) foot and lower leg.
L5
Motor: big toe extension (extensor hallucis longus). Reflex: none reliable. Sensation: top (dorsum) of the foot.
S1
Motor: foot eversion (peroneus longus). Reflex: Achilles (ankle jerk). Sensation: outer (lateral) foot and sole.
Grading
Strength 0 to 5 (5 is normal). Reflexes 0 to 4 (2 is normal).
Babinski
Stroke the outer sole from heel to toes. Normal: toes curl down. Positive: big toe goes up and the other toes fan out = an upper motor neuron (brain or spinal cord) problem.

1

HVLA lumbar spine (Type II): lumbar roll

Technique

What it is for. A quick, short thrust that frees one stuck lumbar joint (a Type II SD). Loosen the muscles with myofascial work first.

Patient
Lying on the side with the posterior TP UP (away from the table).
You
Stand in front of the patient, facing them.
Lock from below
Monitor the level with one hand. Flex the hips and knees until the segment just above the stuck one starts to move.
Legs
The patient straightens the bottom leg. Tuck the top foot into the back of the bottom knee (popliteal fossa).
Lock from above
Pull the bottom shoulder/arm out so the upper body rotates back, away from you, down to the segment just above the stuck one. Thread your arm through to monitor.
Applicator
Cephalad forearm on the front of the shoulder (pectoral area) to hold the upper body. Caudad forearm on the iliac crest and buttock.
Force / direction
Patient breathes in and out; take up the slack. At the end of the breath out, give a quick, short thrust with your caudad forearm, rolling the pelvis forward toward you, in line with the femur, to de-rotate the stuck vertebra.
Finish
Recheck the TP and motion.

Do not use with: Do not thrust with signs of a herniated disc or nerve root damage, fracture, osteoporosis, cancer in the spine, infection, or severe pain on percussion.

3

Muscle energy: quadratus lumborum

Technique

What it is for. Relaxes a tight QL by making it contract gently and then taking up the new length.

Check first
Feel along the bottom of rib 12, the TPs, and the top of the iliac crest for tenderness. Gentle traction on each leg (patient supine) feels stiffer on the tight side. Hip drop is less on the tight side.
Patient
Lying on the side with the tight QL UP, hips and knees slightly bent.
You
Stand behind the patient, at their back.
Contact
One forearm on the top of the iliac crest, the other on the side of the lower rib cage; your fingers rest over the QL to feel it.
Barrier
Spread your forearms apart (pelvis toward the feet, ribs toward the head) until the QL is stretched between rib 12 and the iliac crest.
Contraction
The patient gently "hikes" the hip up toward the shoulder against your equal resistance for 3 to 5 seconds.
Relax and repeat
Patient relaxes; spread your forearms further to the new barrier. Repeat 3 to 5 times.
Other way
Supine leg traction: hold the ankle on the tight side and pull the leg straight down (traction); the patient pulls the leg back up toward the head against you for 3 to 5 seconds; take up the new slack and repeat.
Finish
Passive stretch, then recheck.

3b

Muscle energy: psoas

Technique

What it is for. Relaxes and lengthens a tight psoas (psoas syndrome: bent forward, leaning to one side, positive Thomas test).

Check first
Feel the psoas through the belly. Thomas test: the thigh lifts off the table on the tight side. Passive hip extension is limited.
Patient
Supine at the edge or end of the table in the Thomas-test position: the healthy knee held to the chest, the treated leg hanging off with the hip extended.
You
Stand on the treated side; one hand on the thigh above the knee, the other steadying the pelvis or the held knee.
Barrier
Press the thigh down toward the floor (hip extension) until you feel the psoas tighten.
Contraction
The patient gently lifts the thigh toward the ceiling against your equal resistance for 3 to 5 seconds.
Relax and repeat
Patient relaxes; press the thigh further down to the new barrier. Repeat 3 to 5 times.
Other positions
Prone: bend the knee to 90 degrees, your cephalad hand holds the sacrum down, your other hand lifts the thigh just above the knee into extension; the patient pulls the knee down toward the table against you. Side-lying (if the patient cannot lie prone or at the table end): pull the top leg back into hip extension; the patient pushes the knee forward toward the belly against you.
Finish
Passive stretch, then recheck the Thomas test.

4

Muscle energy: lumbar Type I and Type II (FDR / SUE)

Technique

What it is for. Uses the patient's own gentle push to free a stuck lumbar segment (Type II) or group curve (Type I). Gentler than HVLA.

Type I (group)
Patient lies on the side with the concave side of the curve DOWN (posterior TPs up). You face the patient. Monitor the top of the curve (apex) with your cephalad hand. Flex the hips and knees to about 90 degrees. Lift the ankles toward the ceiling to sidebend the lumbar spine into the barrier. The patient pushes the feet down toward the floor for 3 to 5 seconds against you; relax; lift the feet higher to the new barrier; repeat at least 3 times; finish with a passive stretch.
FDR = Flexed SD
Flexed, Down, Recumbent. The patient lies on the side with the posterior TP DOWN (toward the table); you face the patient and monitor the level with your cephalad hand. (1) Flex the hips and knees until motion reaches the level below the SD. (2) The patient straightens the bottom leg; extend it further until motion reaches the level (this is the extension barrier). Tuck the top foot into the back of the bottom knee. (3) Pull the bottom arm forward and up until rotation reaches the level; the patient can hold the table edge behind them. Hand on the shoulder: as the patient breathes out, follow the shoulder back. (4) Lift the ankle of the top leg toward the ceiling until the lumbar spine sidebends into the barrier. (5) The patient pushes that foot DOWN toward the table for 3 to 5 seconds against you.
SUE = Extended SD
Sims, Up, Extended. The patient lies on the side with the posterior TP UP; you face the patient and monitor the level. (1) Flex the hips and knees until motion reaches the level below the SD (this is the flexion barrier). (2) The patient brings the bottom arm behind and rolls the chest toward the table (the Sims position). Hand on the shoulder: as the patient breathes out and reaches toward the floor, press the shoulder down until rotation reaches the level. (3) Lower both legs off the side of the table until the lumbar spine sidebends into the barrier. (4) The patient pushes the legs UP toward the ceiling for 3 to 5 seconds against you.
Each round
The patient relaxes for 3 to 5 seconds; take up the new sidebending barrier. Repeat 2 more times. Finish with a passive stretch, then recheck the TPs.

5

Muscle energy: piriformis

Technique

What it is for. A tight piriformis holds the hip turned out (externally rotated), limits turning it in, and can irritate the sciatic nerve (buttock pain down the back of the thigh). ME relaxes it so the hip can turn in again.

Check first
Find the piriformis belly (about halfway between the sacrum and the top of the greater trochanter) and press for tenderness and tightness. Compare passive internal rotation of both hips; the tight side turns in less. Direct inhibition first: press steadily on the belly of the muscle until it softens.
Patient
Prone with the knee on the treated side bent to 90 degrees (it can also be done supine).
You
Stand on the treated side holding the ankle/lower leg; your other hand on the buttock over the piriformis.
Barrier
Move the foot outward, away from the midline, which turns the hip IN (internal rotation), until you feel the piriformis tighten.
Contraction
The patient gently pushes the foot back toward the midline (turning the hip out) against your hand on the lower leg for 3 to 5 seconds.
Relax and repeat
Patient relaxes; move the foot further out to the new barrier. Repeat 3 times.
Finish
Passive stretch, then recheck the tender point and internal rotation.

6

Counterstrain: anterior L1

Technique

What it is for. Counterstrain finds a tender point and puts the body in the position where it stops hurting, held 90 seconds, so the reflex muscle spasm resets.

Tender point
Anterior L1 is just medial to the ASIS (go just below the ASIS, then medial). Press until the patient feels it, and call that 10 out of 10.
Patient
Supine.
You
Stand on the side of the tender point. Keep one finger on the point the whole time.
Position
Put your knee up on the table under the patient's legs. Flex both hips and knees up toward the chest, resting the legs on your thigh. Pull the knees toward you (this rotates the pelvis), then pull the ankles toward you (this sidebends toward the tender point). Ask for the number. If it is not at least 70 percent better (3 out of 10 or less), adjust the flexion, rotation and sidebending in small amounts and ask again.
Hold
Hold 90 seconds. The patient stays relaxed.
Return
Bring the legs back slowly and passively (the patient does not help), then press the point again.

Counterstrain: posterior L1 to L5 (tender point on SP or TP)

Technique

What it is for. The same counterstrain idea for tender points on the back of the lumbar spine, mostly treated with extension.

Tender point
On the side or tip of a lumbar SP, or over a TP. Call it 10 out of 10.
Patient
Prone.
SP point
Pure extension: lift both legs straight up off the table and rest them on your thigh until the tenderness is 3 out of 10 or less.
TP point
Ask which side's TP is more tender. Stand on the side OPPOSITE the tender point. Reach across and grab the thigh on the tender-point side just above the knee; lean back to lift it into extension and pull it toward you (adduction), adding a little external rotation, while a finger stays on the tender point. Fine-tune until the tenderness is 3 out of 10 or less.
Hold
Hold 90 seconds while monitoring the point.
Return
Bring the legs back slowly and passively, then recheck the point.
